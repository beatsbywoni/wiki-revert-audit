Dear Editor,

I submit the manuscript "Which reverts count? Reverter populations, attribution rules and observation windows behind a Wikipedia indicator of information quality work" for consideration as a Research Article. It has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. I am the sole author.

**Connection to information science.** JASIST has shaped how the field understands information quality as something constructed through work: the framework of Stvilia et al. (2007), their account of information quality work in Wikipedia (2008), Arazy and Kopak (2011) on the measurability of quality, Mai (2013) on quality as situated, and the systematic review of Wikipedia research by Mesgari et al. (2015). The trace on which much of that empirical tradition now rests, whether an edit was reverted, has been used for a decade as if it were one thing. This manuscript asks what it is an indicator of. It distinguishes three constructs the revert is used to stand for (a quality judgement on an edit, a contributor's experience of rejection, human quality-work effort), shows from a structured review of 34 studies that the operational choices each construct requires are mostly unreported and that the population of reverters is never varied, and then measures those choices on 3.2 million newcomer first edits in seven language editions.

**What is new.** (1) A revert rate is shown to be a mixture: bots, one-click tools, other editors, unregistered editors and the newcomers themselves each contribute a measured share that differs across editions, and the newcomers reverted by each class differ by a factor of 1.4 to 4 in their odds of returning. (2) Excluded reverts are not replaced: follow-up reverts are as frequent after human as after automated first reverts, so post hoc exclusion does not recover a counterfactual. (3) The apparent effect of the observation window reverses between the two attribution rules in common use. (4) MediaWiki's own revert tag omits 70 to 80 percent of reverts made by unregistered editors and by newcomers on editions that patrol recent changes, and almost none elsewhere, which matters for any cross-edition comparison built on the tag. (5) A tiered reporting checklist ties these choices to the construct an analyst intends. The work most closely related in this journal is Gao et al. (2026) on human-bot collaboration in knowledge communities; the present paper supplies the measurement groundwork such studies rely on.

**Registration, stated plainly.** Four editions were explored; hypotheses, thresholds and code were then registered on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23720117) before the outputs of three held-out editions were opened. The manuscript reports all four registered outcomes, including a clause that was rejected and a hypothesis that held in two of three editions, describes the registration as a hold-out replication and not a blind preregistration, and labels as exploratory the analyses designed after the first results. A deviation log is public.

**Generative AI.** Generative AI (Claude, Anthropic) assisted in drafting analysis code and prose and in the first-pass coding of the literature review. I specified the design and the analytic decisions, checked the code against the primary sources cited (the Wikimedia refinery and MediaWiki source code) and against an independent recomputation reported in the manuscript, verified the review coding against the source texts, and take responsibility for every statement. All computation is performed by released deterministic code on unmodified public data; the AI did not modify or by itself analyze data. The same declaration appears in the manuscript.

**Data, code and related work.** The data are public Wikimedia dumps. Code, aggregates, the review table and logs are on GitHub (https://github.com/beatsbywoni/wiki-revert-audit) and archived on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24054641); working drafts in that repository are not a publication. A separate manuscript of mine applies a related audit logic to software repository mining; it uses different data, shares no results with this one, and is not needed to read it.

The body is about 6,800 words including five tables and the captions of four figures, with a 200-word abstract; the Supplementary Material holds nine tables and two figures. I have no funding and no conflicts of interest to declare. Suggested reviewers are entered in the submission system.
