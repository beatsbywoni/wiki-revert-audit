Dear Editor,

I submit the manuscript "Which reverts count? Reverter populations, attribution rules and observation windows behind a Wikipedia indicator of information quality work" for consideration as a Research Article. It has not been published and is not under consideration elsewhere. I am the sole author.

**Connection to information science and to this journal.** The information quality framework of Stvilia et al. (2007), published in this journal, built two of its Wikipedia measures on reverts without saying how a revert was identified or whose reverts were counted. The manuscript starts from that observation and asks what the revert, now a standard trace of information quality work, is an indicator of. A structured review of 41 studies, coded with language-model assistance and reported with its limits, indicates that it stands for four constructs (a quality judgment, conflict, a contributor's experience of rejection, human effort) and that the choices each construct requires are mostly unreported: 31 studies do not say whether bot reverts are counted, 30 state no observation window, and none varies the reverter population. The manuscript then measures those choices on 3.2 million newcomer first edits in seven language editions.

**What is new.** (1) MediaWiki's own revert tag records a revert only when the reverting edit is trusted or patrolled. In the two editions that patrol recent changes it omits 71 and 79 percent of reverts made by unregistered editors, and most reverts by registered accounts that lack patrol-exempt status, against almost none in editions without patrolling; the manuscript tests this directly with the user groups each reverting account held at the time. The consequence is concrete: the difference between English and Italian newcomer revert rates is 3.6 points under a hash-based rule and 7.4 points when measured with the tag. The tag's conditions are documented in the software manual; their size and location in data have not been measured before. (2) A revert rate is a mixture: bots, one-click tools used by a small number of accounts, other editors, unregistered editors and the newcomers themselves each contribute a measured share that differs across editions, and the newcomers reverted by each class differ by a factor of 1.4 to 4 in their odds of returning. (3) Whether excluding a class of reverts matters less with a longer window depends on the attribution rule. (4) A tiered reporting checklist ties the choices to the construct an analyst intends. That reverters differ in kind was known for the English edition; the contribution here is measurement across editions and rules, and I have tried not to claim more.

**Registration.** Four editions were explored; hypotheses, thresholds and code were then registered on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23720117), and three held-out editions were opened afterwards. This is a hold-out replication, not a blind preregistration, and the manuscript says so. All four registered outcomes are reported, including a clause that was rejected and a hypothesis that held in two of three editions. The registered tests concern a filter that, as the review found, published studies do not use; I report them briefly and as registered. The findings listed above as (1) and (2), and the rule dependence in (3), are exploratory and are labeled as such in the abstract, tables and figures. A deviation log is public.

**Generative AI.** Generative AI (Claude, Anthropic) was used for three tasks: drafting analysis code, drafting and editing prose, and first-pass and reliability coding of the literature review, which the manuscript describes together with agreement statistics and its limits (no human second coder was used). Model agents were also used to critique drafts. It was not used to generate, alter or impute data; every reported number is produced by released deterministic code from unmodified public dumps. I specified the design, made the analytic decisions, checked the code and the review coding against the sources, and take responsibility for every statement. The same declaration appears in the manuscript, and a dated log of AI assistance is in the repository.

**Data, code and related work.** The data are public Wikimedia dumps. Code, aggregates, the review table and logs are on GitHub (https://github.com/beatsbywoni/wiki-revert-audit) and archived on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24054641). A separate manuscript of mine applies a related audit logic to software repository mining; it uses different data and shares no results with this one.

The body is about 7,000 words including five tables, figure captions and endnotes, with a 200-word abstract; the Supplementary Material holds the registration account, ten tables and two figures.
